AI/ML Tutor: Designing, Building, and Testing an Explain-Then-Quiz Chatbot

Introduction

In the AI Lab for AIML-500, I explored prompt engineering and AI tools through three activities: comparing one prompt across three large language models, working with a custom GPT tutor, and generating a researched article with STORM. I then used what I learned to design, build, and test my own AI/ML tutor bot in Mizou. It addresses a common problem for beginners: explanations pitched at the wrong level, with no way to check understanding.

Description

The AI/ML Tutor is a chatbot that teaches AI/ML basics one concept at a time. It greets the learner, says it is an AI that can make mistakes, and asks what topic they want and how much they already know. For a broad request, it offers subtopics. It then explains one concept in plain language with an example and asks one quiz question at a time. It adapts to the learner’s answers, getting harder after a correct answer and simpler after a wrong one. It closes with a summary, one thing to review, and a suggested next concept.

Objective

Beginners who want to learn AI/ML basics need clear explanations at their own level and a way to check their understanding. General chat tools often assume too much or too little and do not confirm that the learner understood. My goal was to build a tutor that explains first, quizzes second, and adapts to the learner, while staying honest about its limits. I also wanted to learn how prompt structure and wording change an AI’s output, and how to judge that output critically.

Process

I followed a design-thinking process.

  1. Groundwork. Comparing Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini showed me that thorough responses can break explicit constraints. Testing the Tutor Me GPT showed me what a tutor that waits for answers feels like. Checking STORM’s article claim by claim reinforced that AI output needs verification.
  2. Empathize. I identified learners’ pain points: explanations that are too advanced or too simple, no way to check understanding, confident but wrong answers, and tools that give answers instead of teaching. I wrote a persona: Maya, 28, who works full time, has no coding background, and has about 30 minutes a few evenings a week.
  3. Define and ideate. I brainstormed three ideas: a concept explainer, an explain-then-quiz tutor, and a jargon translator. I rated each on impact, feasibility, and user value, and chose the explain-then-quiz tutor because it directly addresses the lack of a way to check understanding.
  4. Prototype. I built the bot in Mizou’s Custom mode. I replaced the quiz template’s instructions with my own, condensed to fit the 1,000-character limit (987 used).
  5. Test, tune, iterate. I tested 13 scenarios, including vague topics, wrong answers, off-topic requests, homework requests, and personal information. I also ran three ethical checks: a nervous non-native-English learner, a stereotype-style question, and a medical question.

The known limitation: when a learner pastes the quiz question back, the bot reveals the answer. I tried two instruction fixes and documented the limitation rather than hiding it. The two partial passes were a wrong-answer follow-up that reused the same example, and a missing warning when the learner shared personal details.

Tips if you build a bot like this

  • Enter your instructions in the bot’s settings, not the chat box.
  • Write instructions to fit the character limit from the start.
  • Check that leftover template text doesn’t conflict with your instructions.
  • Test each rule with a specific scenario, including edge cases like a learner pasting the question back.

Introduction

Choosing between traditional machine learning and deep learning is one of the first decisions in an AI project, and a more powerful model is not always the better one. This artifact compares when each approach fits, using two real-world cases.

Description

A short analytical paper comparing Apache SpamAssassin (traditional ML for spam detection) with deep learning for diabetic retinopathy screening from retinal images. For each case, I explain why the chosen approach fits and why the other would not.

Simple MLDeep learning
Who picks the featuresA personThe network, from raw data
Best data typeStructured, easy-to-define featuresComplex, unstructured (images)
Cost and speedQuick and cheap to train and retrainMore data, computing power, and tuning
ExplainabilityEasy to trace why a result happenedHarder to explain
Case in my paperSpam detection (SpamAssassin)Diabetic retinopathy screening

Objective

Show that I can evaluate a problem’s data type, need for explainability, and risk level, and then recommend an appropriate AI approach.

Process

  1. Define the difference. The core difference is who selects the features: a person in ML, the network in deep learning.
  2. Choose examples. I chose one structured-data example (spam filtering) and one unstructured-data example (medical imaging).
  3. Research. I used peer-reviewed and official sources, including Nature, JAMA, and npj Digital Medicine.
  4. Compare. I compared each case on data type, computing cost, explainability, and the consequences of errors.
  5. Revise. I revised the draft for clarity and cited all sources in APA format.

Tools and technologies used

  • Claude (Anthropic): clarifying concepts and proofreading, with all analysis and conclusions my own
  • Scholarly journals and databases: source research
  • APA citation style: references
  • Concepts covered: logistic regression, decision trees, convolutional neural networks, feature engineering

Value proposition

Unique value

I explain a technical topic in plain language, with real examples and credible sources, so people new to AI can follow it. I also make the point that the most advanced model is not always the best choice.

Relevance

For classmates, it works as a quick reference on when to use ML versus deep learning. For my professor, it shows I can apply course concepts to real systems (SpamAssassin, IDx-DR) and support my claims with research.

Introduction

Knowing when to trust an AI tool is as important as knowing how to use one. In AIML-500, I completed Professor Predict, an interactive exercise where an AI guide walks learners through how large language models are trained and what that means for what they can and cannot do reliably.

Description

Professor Predict is a four-stage exercise followed by a personalized summary. In Be the Model, I completed partial sentences from gut-level patterns. In Reveal the Mechanism, the guide explained next-token prediction and why fluent text is not the same as verified truth. In Guess the Training Mix, I sorted kinds of material by how much of it models likely learn from. In Good Fit / Danger Zone, I decided which tasks a model handles well and which need a source, tool, or check.

The key moment came in the first stage. For a fictional country, Velmora, I answered “president.” When asked whether I knew the answer or generated something that only sounded like one, I admitted it only sounded like an answer. That is a small version of hallucination.

Objective

Connect how language models are trained to their real-world strengths and limits, and be able to say when an AI output needs a source, a tool, or human verification, and why.

Process

  1. Read. I read “A Beginner’s Guide to Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning” (Jones, 2021) for background.
  2. Run the exercise. I pasted the course prompt into an AI tool and worked through the four stages one question at a time.
  3. Reason out loud. I explained my choices, which is how the guide caught my mistakes.
  4. Capture. I saved the concluding summary the exercise produced.
  5. Revise. I turned the chat into this case study and added the table above so a reader can see my reasoning and my corrections at a glance.

Tools and technologies used

  • Claude (Anthropic): ran the Professor Predict exercise and supported editing of this write-up
  • IBM Developer article (Jones, 2021): background reading on AI and machine learning

Value proposition

Unique value

This artifact shows that I apply a quality engineer’s habit of verification to AI: asking what an output is based on and what could check it. I also document my mistakes openly, which is how real testing improves a system.

Relevance

Teams adopting AI tools need people who can tell a good use from a risky one. I can help decide where AI is safe to use, where it needs a tool or a human check, and how to test it.
